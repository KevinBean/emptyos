Case study

Six vowels, four letters, one spelling

⟨ough⟩ is pronounced at least ten different ways.2 Six of them are distinct vowels; the rest bring a consonant along. Tough, though, through and thorough are each one letter longer than the last, and no two rhyme.

Why

The spelling stopped moving and the sound didn't

None of this was designed. Between roughly 1400 and 1700, every long vowel in English shifted position in the mouth — the Great Vowel Shift.4 The vowel in bite had been closer to today's beet; the vowel in meet had been closer to mate. The whole system rotated.

Caxton set up England's first printing press in 1476 — squarely mid-shift.4 Printers standardised on the spellings they already had, and by the time pronunciation settled in the seventeenth century there were too many books in circulation to renegotiate.

English spelling is a photograph of how the language sounded before it finished changing.

So the graph above isn't noise, it's sediment. ⟨ea⟩ in great, head and eat once shared a vowel. ⟨gh⟩ in night was pronounced. Silent ⟨e⟩ was a spoken syllable. Each stranded spelling marks a sound that moved out from under it.

The consolation is that it is messier than phonetic but tidier than its reputation. When Hanna and colleagues ran 17,310 words through a sound-to-spelling algorithm in 1966, half came out spelled correctly from sound alone, and 84% were wrong by at most one letter.3 The system is about four-fifths regular. It's the last fifth that contains most of the common words.
